Commercial Cleaning Contracts: What You're Actually Paying For

Signing a commercial cleaning contract can feel like a formality, but the details buried in those pages determine what you actually receive — and what you don't. Before you put pen to paper, here's what every office manager should understand about cleaning service agreements.

The Scope of Work Section Is Everything

The most important part of any commercial cleaning contract is the scope of work. This section should detail every task the cleaning crew is expected to perform, how often, and in which areas of your facility. Vague language like “general cleaning” is a red flag — look for itemized task lists broken down by room or zone. See our guide on how to write an office cleaning scope for what a specific, useful scope actually looks like.

Common Line Items in an Office Cleaning Contract

  • Vacuuming and mopping of all floors (specify types: carpet, tile, hardwood)
  • Restroom sanitization and restocking (paper, soap, bags)
  • Trash and recycling removal
  • Breakroom and kitchen cleaning
  • Surface and desk wipe-downs
  • Window and glass cleaning (interior or exterior?)
  • Floor buffing or waxing (and how often?)

What's Usually Not Included

Most standard contracts exclude biohazard cleanup, post-construction cleaning, exterior pressure washing, deep carpet extraction, and pest control. If you expect any of these occasionally, ask for a supplemental rate schedule upfront.

Watch for These Contract Clauses

Auto-renewal clauses: Many contracts automatically renew for 12 months unless you give 30–60 days' written notice. Price escalation clauses: Some contracts allow annual price increases tied to cost-of-living or labor cost changes. Termination fees: Leaving early may cost you several months of remaining contract value. Complaint procedures: How do you report a missed clean? What's the remedy timeline?

Short-Term vs. Long-Term Contracts

Month-to-month contracts offer flexibility but typically cost more per visit. Annual contracts lock in pricing and are better for budget predictability. A trial period is a reasonable ask if you want to test a new provider before committing long-term.
